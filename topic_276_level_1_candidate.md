## 1. The idea of a tail event

Imagine flipping a fair coin over and over forever. Some questions depend on the first few flips, such as “was the first flip heads?” Other questions depend on what happens far out in the sequence, such as “do heads occur infinitely often?” Changing a finite number of early flips cannot change the answer to the second question. It is a **tail event**.

Kolmogorov’s 0–1 law says that if the random variables in the sequence are independent, every tail event has probability either 0 or 1. It cannot have a probability such as 0.4. The law does not tell us which of the two answers applies; it only rules out probabilities strictly between them.

## 2. The sigma-field language, one step at a time

A sigma-field is a collection of events for which probabilities and set operations are defined. The notation $\sigma(X_n,X_{n+1},\ldots)$ means the information contained in the variables from $X_n$ onward.

For each $n$, form the sigma-field generated by the sequence starting at $n$:

$$
\mathcal{T}_n=\sigma(X_n,X_{n+1},X_{n+2},\ldots).
$$

As $n$ increases, we remove more of the beginning. The tail sigma-field is the information that remains no matter how many initial variables are removed:

$$
\mathcal{T}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{T}_n.
$$

An event $A$ in $\mathcal{T}$ can be determined from the sequence after every finite starting point. That is the formal version of “changing any finite number of initial outcomes does not change whether $A$ occurs.”

For example, “the first flip is heads” is not a tail event: changing the first result changes the answer. “Heads occur infinitely often” is a tail event: changing five or five thousand initial flips cannot change whether heads continue to appear infinitely many times in the remaining sequence. A tail event can depend on infinitely many outcomes; it simply cannot depend on any fixed finite beginning.

The sigma-field $\sigma(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$ means all events that can be described using the first $n$ variables. As $n$ grows, these collections contain more and more information. Their union generates the information from the entire sequence. This lets the proof move from independence of a finite beginning to independence from the whole sequence.

## 3. Why the probability must be zero or one

Let $A$ be a tail event. Look first at the first $n$ variables, $X_1,\ldots,X_n$. Since the variables are independent, the information from those first $n$ variables is independent of the later variables. The event $A$ belongs to the information in the sequence after $n$, so $A$ is independent of every event determined by the first $n$ variables.

Now let $n$ grow. The first 1 variable, then the first 2, then the first 3, and so on eventually describe the whole sequence. Independence carries over to events determined by the full sequence. Because $A$ is itself determined by the full sequence, this means $A$ is independent of itself.

If an event is independent of itself, then

$$
P(A\cap A)=P(A)P(A).
$$

But $A\cap A=A$, so if $p=P(A)$,

$$
p=p^2.
$$

The only solutions are $p=0$ and $p=1$. That is the whole reason for the name “0–1 law.” Independence is essential to this reasoning; without it, a tail event can have an intermediate probability.

Here is the extension step in more detail. Let $\mathcal{F}_n=\sigma(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$ and let $\mathcal{F}_{\infty}=\sigma(X_1,X_2,\ldots)$. For every $n$, $A$ belongs to $\sigma(X_{n+1},X_{n+2},\ldots)$ and so is independent of $\mathcal{F}_n$. Events depending on any finite number of coordinates build up the full sigma-field $\mathcal{F}_{\infty}$. Independence extends from those finite-coordinate events to the sigma-field they generate. Therefore $A$ is independent of $\mathcal{F}_{\infty}$. Since $A$ itself belongs to $\mathcal{F}_{\infty}$, it is independent of itself. This is not an assumption that $A$ and $A$ are different events; it follows precisely because $A$ can be described from the whole sequence.

A quick comparison before submitting is: the 0–1 law starts with an independent sequence and a tail event, then concludes the event probability is zero or one; Borel–Cantelli starts with a sequence of events and a sum of their probabilities, then concludes whether infinitely many occurrences have probability zero or one. The assumptions are not interchangeable. In particular, a tail event can be identified without knowing its probability, while a Borel–Cantelli conclusion requires checking the relevant series and, for the divergent case, independence of the events.
If $p=P(A)$, independence gives $P(A\cap A)=P(A)P(A)=p^2$. But intersection with itself leaves the same event, so $P(A\cap A)=P(A)=p$. Hence $p(1-p)=0$. Since probabilities lie between 0 and 1, the only possibilities are 0 and 1. This proof does not tell which outcome applies; an additional argument is needed to decide whether a particular tail event has probability zero or one.

## 4. Example: infinitely many heads

Let $X_n=1$ when the $n$th fair coin flip is heads and $X_n=0$ when it is tails. The event

$$
A=\{\text{heads occur infinitely often}\}
$$

does not depend on any finite set of early flips: after changing the first 20 results, for example, there are still infinitely many flips left, and the question is whether heads continue to occur infinitely often in the remainder. Thus $A$ is a tail event. Kolmogorov’s law tells us $P(A)$ must be zero or one.

For fair independent flips, the second Borel–Cantelli lemma shows it is one, because the probability of heads at each flip is $1/2$ and the sum $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/2$ diverges. The 0–1 law alone does not choose between zero and one; Borel–Cantelli supplies the additional calculation in this example.

For a direct check, choose a starting flip $m$. The chance of no heads from flip $m$ through flip $n$ is $(1/2)^{n-m+1}$. As $n$ increases, this chance tends to zero. Thus the probability that there are no heads after any fixed starting point is zero. “Only finitely many heads” means there is some last starting point after which no heads occur; it is the union over all possible $m$ of these zero-probability events, so it also has probability zero. The complementary event, infinitely many heads, has probability one.

A related event is “eventually every flip is heads.” This is also a tail event, so the 0–1 law says its probability is zero or one. But it is zero: after any fixed time, the probability of getting heads for the next $m$ flips is $(1/2)^m$, which tends to zero as $m$ grows. Taking the countable union over all possible starting times still gives probability zero.

## 5. Borel–Cantelli: a connected but separate result

For events $A_1,A_2,\ldots$, the event that infinitely many occur is written

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_n.
$$

The first Borel–Cantelli statement says that if

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(A_n)<\infty,
$$

then $P(\limsup A_n)=0$. Independence is not needed for this part. Intuitively, the total amount of probability in the far-out events becomes arbitrarily small, so the chance that events keep occurring forever is zero.

The second statement says that if the events are independent and

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(A_n)=\infty,
$$

then $P(\limsup A_n)=1$. Here independence is needed. A simple example is independent events with the same probability $c>0$: the sum diverges, so the events occur infinitely often with probability one.

The role of independence can be seen by looking at a block of events from $m$ to $n$. The probability that none occurs is

$$
P(A_m^c\cap\cdots\cap A_n^c)
=\prod_{k=m}^{n}(1-P(A_k)).
$$

Since $1-x\le e^{-x}$ for $0\le x\le1$, this product is at most

$$
\exp\left(-\sum_{k=m}^{n}P(A_k)\right).
$$

If the sum of probabilities diverges, this upper bound tends to zero as $n$ grows. Therefore, after any fixed time, it is certain that at least one of the events eventually occurs. Applying this to every starting point shows that infinitely many events occur with probability one.

The infinitely-often event is a tail event, which is why the 0–1 law is relevant. But the laws are not identical: Kolmogorov says a tail-event probability is 0 or 1; Borel–Cantelli gives conditions on the event probabilities that determine which value occurs.

The first Borel–Cantelli statement has a shorter proof and does not use independence. For any starting point $m$, the probability that at least one event occurs from $m$ onward is at most the sum of their probabilities:

$$
P\left(\bigcup_{k=m}^{\infty}A_k\right)
\le \sum_{k=m}^{\infty}P(A_k).
$$

If the full series is finite, its tail on the right tends to zero as $m$ grows. The event that infinitely many $A_k$ occur is contained in this union for every $m$, so its probability must be zero. For example, if $P(A_n)=2^{-n}$, the sum is finite and only finitely many events occur almost surely, whether or not the events are independent.

## 6. Revision checks and common mistakes

- **Independence:** the sequence of random variables must be independent.
- **Tail event:** the event must be unaffected by changing finitely many initial variables.
- **Conclusion:** the probability is either 0 or 1, not necessarily 1.
- **Proof step:** establish independence from every finite initial segment, then extend to the sigma-field generated by the whole sequence.
- **Self-independence:** since the tail event belongs to the whole-sequence sigma-field, it is independent of itself; then $p=p^2$.
- **Borel–Cantelli I:** a finite sum of event probabilities implies only finitely many occurrences almost surely; no independence is required.
- **Borel–Cantelli II:** a divergent sum plus independence implies infinitely many occurrences almost surely.

Common mistakes are assuming every event has probability zero or one (only tail events do), applying the law to a dependent sequence, claiming the law itself tells whether the probability is zero or one, and adding independence to the first Borel–Cantelli statement when it is not needed. Keep the event, the independence assumption, and the conclusion separate in a proof.
