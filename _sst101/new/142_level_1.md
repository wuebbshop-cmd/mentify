## 1. Foundational Concepts, Definitions, and Axioms

### 1.1 A stall, a notebook, and one awkward question

Imagine you sell vegetables at a busy market and you want to know how much a typical household in your town spends on vegetables each week. You could stand at your stall and ask every single household, but there are thousands of them, many never visit your stall, and by the time you finished asking, the season would have changed and your answer would describe last season rather than this one. So you do something cleverer. You choose a manageable group of households, ask them carefully, write the answers in a notebook, and then use those answers to say something sensible about the whole town.

That is data collection. Nothing in statistics matters more, because every average, every table, and every conclusion you ever compute is built on numbers that somebody had to gather first. A beautifully calculated average of badly gathered numbers is still a wrong answer, and no amount of arithmetic can repair it afterwards.

Picture the same idea in a second setting. A school wants to know whether a new revision timetable helps learners. The head teacher cannot simply ask learners whether they improved, because learners may not know. Someone has to decide what to measure, who to measure it on, and how to measure it without distorting what happens. Again, this is data collection, and again the decision made at this stage controls what can honestly be claimed at the end.

Think of the whole subject as a chain with four links: collect, organise, summarise, and conclude. The first link is the one studied here. The later links are the frequency tables, averages, and probability statements that fill the rest of the course.

### 1.2 Statistics, and the two jobs it does

Statistics is the science of data. In its full sense it involves collecting, classifying, summarising, organising, analysing, and interpreting numerical information.

The subject does two genuinely different jobs, and the difference is one of intention rather than of technique.

> **Definition (Descriptive statistics).** Descriptive statistics uses numerical and graphical methods to look for patterns in a data set and to present that information in a convenient form.

> **Definition (Inferential statistics).** Inferential statistics uses sample data to make estimates, decisions, predictions, or other generalisations about a larger set of data.

Descriptive work stops with the data in hand: it counts, tabulates, averages, and displays. Inferential work reaches past the data in hand: it uses a measured group to say something about an unmeasured whole, and it attaches a statement of how much trust that generalisation deserves.

The distinction decides how you must word a conclusion. Saying "the 200 households we asked spent an average of 1 250 shillings last week" is a descriptive statement and is completely safe. Saying "households in this town spend about 1 250 shillings a week" is an inference, and it must be qualified, because the 200 households were chosen in some particular way and the other thousands were not asked at all.

### 1.3 The five elements that frame every collection exercise

Every data collection problem, no matter how it is worded, is built from five elements. Learn them as vocabulary, because every later question is posed in these words.

- A **population** is the set of all units, usually people, objects, transactions, or events, that we are interested in studying. In the market problem the population is every household in the town. A population is fixed by the question, not by its size; it might be forty learners in one class or every registered voter in a country.
- A **variable** is a characteristic or property of an individual population unit. Weekly vegetable spending is a variable. So is the number of children in a family, a person's opinion of a bus service, and the mass of a bag of maize.
- A **sample** is a subset of the units of a population. The two hundred households you actually asked form the sample. We use a sample because measuring everything is often impossible, too expensive, or destructive; you cannot test the strength of every brick in a wall by breaking it.
- A **statistical inference** is an estimate, prediction, or other generalisation about a population based on information contained in a sample. "The town average is about 1 250 shillings" is the inference.
- A **measure of reliability** is a statement, usually attached to a statistical inference, that says how much confidence the inference deserves. "Give or take 90 shillings" is a measure of reliability, and so is the phrase "with 95 per cent confidence".

The chain joining them never changes. A variable is measured on a sample drawn from a population; an inference is formed about the population; a measure of reliability states how far the inference can be trusted.

### 1.4 Primary data and secondary data

There is a second division that cuts across all five methods, and beginners very often miss it.

**Primary data** are data collected by the person who is going to use them, for the purpose in hand. If you stand at your stall with your own notebook and ask your own questions, the data are primary. You know exactly what was asked, of whom, when, and how the answers were recorded, because you did all of it.

**Secondary data** are data collected by somebody else, for some other purpose, and then used by you. If you copy the average household spending figure from a national household survey report, the data are secondary. You gain enormously in cost and time, since the work is already done, but you lose control: you did not write the questions, you may not know how the respondents were chosen, and the definitions used may not match the definition you need.

A simple test settles the classification in any exam question. Ask who did the collecting and why. If the answer is "the researcher, for this study", the data are primary. If the answer is "somebody else, for a different purpose", the data are secondary.

### 1.5 The five collection methods

The five methods named for this topic are surveys, experiments, observational studies, interviews, and questionnaires. They are easiest to keep apart by asking two questions of each: who or what is being measured, and how much does the researcher control the conditions?

**A survey** asks a defined group of respondents a set of questions in order to describe their characteristics, attitudes, or behaviour. The researcher records what respondents say; nothing is manipulated. A survey of households about weekly vegetable spending is a survey.

**An experiment** deliberately imposes a treatment or condition on the units and observes the result. The researcher does not merely watch; the researcher acts. If you give one group of shoppers a discount voucher and another group nothing, and then compare their spending, you have run an experiment.

**An observational study** records units as they naturally are, without imposing any treatment. If you simply record the spending of shoppers who happen to receive vouchers from a promotion you did not design, you have run an observational study.

**An interview** is a direct conversation in which a researcher questions a respondent, usually face to face or by telephone, and records the answers. Its defining advantage is that the researcher can clarify a question, probe an answer, and follow an unexpected line of thought.

**A questionnaire** is a written instrument, a fixed set of questions that respondents complete themselves or that an interviewer reads out and records. Its defining advantage is standardisation: every respondent meets the same words in the same order, so the answers can be compared.

These five are not rivals. A survey is the overall design; the questionnaire is the instrument that carries it out; interviews may be the way the questionnaire is administered to respondents who cannot read. A single investigation may use a questionnaire to reach eight hundred people and then interview thirty of them in depth, so that breadth and depth are both obtained.

### 1.6 The four routes by which data are obtained

There is a shorter, older classification that every textbook uses, and it is worth memorising alongside the five methods because it organises them.

- **Data from a published source.** Somebody else has already collected and published the numbers: a statistical abstract, a ministry report, a company's annual record, a journal article.
- **Data from a designed experiment.** The researcher sets the conditions, assigns the treatments, and records the responses.
- **Data from a survey.** The researcher questions a sample of respondents.
- **Data collected observationally.** The researcher watches and records without intervening.

The mapping between the two lists is direct. Published-source data are secondary data. The designed-experiment route corresponds to experiments. The survey route corresponds to surveys, and interviews and questionnaires are the two instruments by which survey responses are elicited. The observational route corresponds to observational studies.

The route matters more than the label, because the route determines what may be concluded. A designed experiment can support a statement about cause and effect, since the researcher controlled who received which treatment. A survey or an observational study generally cannot, because other differences between the groups may explain the difference in outcome.

### 1.7 Two kinds of data, and four levels of measurement

Data are of two broad kinds.

- **Quantitative data** are measurements recorded on a naturally occurring numerical scale: weekly spending, number of children, mass in kilograms, examination mark.
- **Qualitative data** are measurements that cannot be measured on a natural numerical scale and can only be classified into one of a group of categories: sex, religion, employment status, preferred means of transport.

Within these two kinds, four levels of measurement decide which arithmetic is meaningful.

- **Nominal** data are qualitative measurements that differ only in name, with no order; a list of favourite colours is nominal, and averaging the colours is meaningless.
- **Ordinal** data are qualitative measurements with a meaningful order but no meaningful distance; a satisfaction rating of poor, fair, good, excellent is ordinal, and the gap between fair and good need not equal the gap between good and excellent.
- **Interval** data are quantitative measurements with equal distances between consecutive values but an arbitrary zero; temperature in degrees Celsius is interval, because zero degrees does not mean "no temperature".
- **Ratio** data are quantitative measurements with equal distances and a true zero, so that ratios are meaningful; mass, income, and spending are ratio measurements, and a household spending twice another really does spend twice as much.

This matters at the design stage rather than at the analysis stage. If you write a questionnaire item that records income in broad bands, you have created ordinal data and you have thrown away the ability to compute a trustworthy mean. Choose the measurement level deliberately, because no later summary can recover information the instrument never captured.

### 1.8 The three working rules of the collection stage

Three rules govern the collection stage itself. They are stated as axioms here because everything later depends on them, and because they are assumed rather than proved.

> **Rule 1 (The completeness identity).** Every unit in the sample must contribute exactly one usable record, so that the number of records equals the number of units selected. If $n$ units are selected and $r$ usable records are obtained, then the response rate is the ratio of $r$ to $n$.

Here $n$ is the number of units the researcher selected, $r$ is the number of units that produced a usable record, and the response rate is the fraction that did respond. If nine out of ten selected households answer, the response rate is nine tenths, that is 90 per cent.

> **Rule 2 (The single-class rule).** Every observation must fall into exactly one category of every classification applied to it. A respondent who is recorded as both "employed" and "unemployed", or an age recorded as both "under 30" and "30 to 40", makes every frequency table built from the data wrong.

> **Rule 3 (The neutrality rule).** The instrument must not push the respondent towards an answer. A question that suggests a preferred reply, or an interviewer who reacts to answers, contaminates the data at source, and no statistical method removes contamination introduced in this way.

Each rule protects a later calculation. Rule 1 protects the frequency total, because the frequencies in a table can only sum to the number of records actually collected. Rule 2 protects every table and every average, because overlapping categories double count. Rule 3 protects the interpretation, because a biased instrument produces a precise but wrong summary.

### 1.9 Two words that decide whether an instrument is any good

Two properties are asked of every instrument, and they are not the same property.

**Reliability** is consistency. A reliable instrument gives nearly the same answer when it is used again on the same unchanged thing. **Validity** is correctness of purpose. A valid instrument measures the characteristic it claims to measure.

A bathroom scale that adds two kilograms to every reading is perfectly reliable, because it gives the same wrong answer every morning, and it is not valid. A scale that gives a different reading each time you step on it is neither reliable nor valid, because random wobble destroys both. Reliability is therefore necessary but not sufficient for validity: an instrument must be consistent before it can be correct, but consistency alone guarantees nothing.

This distinction is the practical reason a questionnaire is piloted before it is used at scale. Piloting reveals whether respondents understand the words in the same way, whether the answer options cover the real replies, and whether the same respondent gives similar answers on two occasions.

## 2. Theorems, Lemmas, and Analytical Properties

### 2.1 The response rate

The response rate is the first quantity to compute in any survey question, because every later statement about representativeness depends on it.

> **Result (Response rate).** If $n$ units are selected and $r$ of them produce a usable response, the response rate is the fraction

$$
\text{response rate} = \frac{r}{n} .
$$

The symbol $r$ counts the usable responses only; a returned sheet with every question left blank is not a response, and neither is a returned sheet that arrived after the deadline if the deadline was part of the design. The symbol $n$ counts the units that were actually eligible and contactable, so undelivered letters and disconnected telephone numbers are removed from $n$ before the division is performed. Multiplying the fraction by 100 turns it into a percentage, which is how it is normally reported.

A worked illustration uses small numbers. If letters are posted to 500 households and 380 are delivered to a valid address, with 285 returned completed, then

$$
\text{response rate} = \frac{285}{380} = 0.75 ,
$$

which is 75 per cent. Dividing by 500 instead would give 57 per cent, and that figure would mix two different failures, bad addresses and refusals, into one number and so hide which failure needs attention.

### 2.2 Bias created by the people who do not respond

The response rate matters because non-respondents are usually not like respondents.

> **Result (Non-response bias).** If a fraction $w$ of the selected sample does not respond, and the non-respondents differ from the respondents by an average amount $d$, then the mean computed from respondents alone is displaced from the true sample mean by the product of the two.

In words: the bias equals the share who did not answer, multiplied by how different those people are. Writing the share who did respond as $w_r$ and the share who did not as $w_n$, with the two shares adding to one, the bias in the respondent mean is

$$
\text{bias} = w_n \left(\bar{x}_{n} - \bar{x}_{r}\right),
$$

where $\bar{x}_{r}$ is the mean of the respondents, $\bar{x}_{n}$ is the mean of the non-respondents, and $w_n$ is the fraction of the sample that did not respond. The expression in brackets is the gap between the two groups, and the fraction outside it is the weight that gap carries in the final figure.

The result has an encouraging consequence and a warning. The consequence is that a large non-response share is harmless when non-respondents are just like respondents, because the gap is then near zero. The warning is that a small non-response share is dangerous when the gap is large, so a survey reporting a 95 per cent response rate is not automatically safe.

### 2.3 Total survey error as a sum of parts

Four different faults can spoil a survey, and they are added rather than confused.

> **Result (Bias decomposition).** The error of a survey estimate has four components: coverage error, arising because the list of units from which the sample was drawn omits part of the population; sampling error, arising because a sample rather than the whole population was measured; non-response error, arising because selected units did not supply data; and measurement error, arising because the answers recorded differ from the truth.

In symbols, writing the four components in the same order,

$$
\text{total error} = \text{coverage} + \text{sampling} + \text{non-response} + \text{measurement} .
$$

Each component answers a different question. Coverage asks whether every member of the population had a chance of selection. Sampling asks how much the estimate would move if a different sample of the same size had been drawn. Non-response asks whether the people who answered resemble the people who did not. Measurement asks whether the answers, once given, are accurate.

The four are treated differently. Sampling error shrinks as the sample grows, and it can be quantified. The other three do not shrink reliably as the sample grows; a larger sample of a badly covered frame simply produces a more confident version of the same mistake.

### 2.4 Why randomisation makes a comparison fair

An experiment compares groups that differ in one deliberate respect, the treatment, and should differ in nothing else.

> **Result (Randomisation).** If units are assigned to treatments at random, then every other characteristic of the units is balanced between the groups in expectation, whether or not the researcher thought to measure it.

"Balance in expectation" means that if the same randomisation were repeated many times, the average difference between the groups in any characteristic would be zero. In a single experiment the balance is approximate, not exact, which is why randomisation is paired with replication: with more units, the accidental imbalance between groups becomes smaller.

Randomisation does something no amount of careful measurement can do. It balances characteristics the researcher never recorded, including characteristics nobody has thought of, because the coin does not know what is being balanced.

### 2.5 Replication and the square-root law of precision

**Replication** means applying each treatment to several independent units rather than to one.

> **Result (Square-root law).** For a mean of $n$ independent observations, the standard error is proportional to one over the square root of $n$, so quadrupling the sample halves the standard error.

The standard error here is the typical distance between a sample mean and the population mean it estimates; a small standard error means a precise estimate. Writing the standard deviation of a single observation as $\sigma$, the standard error of the mean of $n$ observations is

$$
\text{SE} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} .
$$

The symbol $\sigma$ is the spread of individual values around their mean, and $\sqrt{n}$ is the square root of the number of observations. Because the square root grows slowly, precision is expensive: to halve the standard error, you must quadruple the sample.

### 2.6 Blocking: using a known nuisance to sharpen a comparison

**Blocking** means grouping units that are alike in some characteristic that affects the response, and then comparing the treatments inside each group.

> **Result (Blocking).** When the blocks explain part of the variation in the response, comparing treatments within blocks gives a smaller standard error for the treatment difference than comparing treatments across the whole set of units.

The reasoning is that variation between blocks is removed from the comparison before the treatments are compared. If four shops differ greatly in size, and each shop receives both a new display and the old display in turn, the comparison is made inside each shop, where size is held constant by construction.

The efficiency gained can be measured. Writing the variance of a single observation as $\sigma^{2}$ and the fraction of that variance explained by the blocks as $\rho$, the variance of the treatment difference is reduced by roughly the factor

$$
1 - \rho ,
$$

where $\rho$ is a number between zero and one describing how strongly the blocks predict the response. When the blocks explain half the variation, so that $\rho = 0.5$, the variance of the difference is halved and the standard error is divided by about 1.41.

### 2.7 Confounding: the identity behind an unfair comparison

An observational study compares groups that were not formed by the researcher, so the groups may differ in many ways at once.

> **Result (Confounding identity).** An observed difference between two groups equals the effect of the factor of interest plus the difference contributed by every other factor on which the groups differ.

Writing the observed difference as $D$, the causal effect of interest as $\tau$, and the contribution of the confounders as $C$,

$$
D = \tau + C .
$$

The symbol $D$ is what the data show, $\tau$ is what an experiment would reveal, and $C$ is the distortion produced by the imbalance in other factors. Because a single equation contains two unknowns, $D$ alone cannot pin down $\tau$; the causal effect is not identified unless $C$ can be argued to be zero, which is precisely what randomisation achieves.

### 2.8 Reliability: observed score as truth plus error

Classical measurement theory models every recorded answer as a true value plus a random error.

> **Result (Classical test theory).** The observed score equals the true score plus measurement error, and the reliability of an instrument is the share of the observed variance that is due to true differences.

Writing the observed score as $X$, the true score as $T$, and the error as $E$,

$$
X = T + E .
$$

The symbol $X$ is what the instrument records, $T$ is the value that would be recorded by a perfect instrument, and $E$ is the error, assumed to average zero and to be unrelated to $T$. Reliability is then

$$
\rho_{XX} = \frac{\sigma_{T}^{2}}{\sigma_{T}^{2} + \sigma_{E}^{2}} ,
$$

where $\sigma_{T}^{2}$ is the variance of the true scores and $\sigma_{E}^{2}$ is the variance of the errors. The ratio lies between zero and one; a value near one means the instrument mostly records truth, and a value near zero means it mostly records noise.

### 2.9 Validity: measuring the thing you named

**Validity** is the extent to which an instrument measures the characteristic it is intended to measure.

> **Result (Attenuation).** An unreliable instrument weakens every relationship it is used to study, so an observed association is smaller in magnitude than the true association.

Writing the observed correlation as $r_{\text{obs}}$, the true correlation as $r_{\text{true}}$, and the reliability as $\rho_{XX}$,

$$
r_{\text{obs}} = r_{\text{true}} \sqrt{\rho_{XX}} .
$$

The square root of the reliability is the factor by which the truth is shrunk. If the true correlation is $0.6$ and the reliability is $0.64$, whose square root is $0.8$, then the observed correlation is $0.48$. This is why a badly designed questionnaire can make a real effect look absent, and why a low correlation is not by itself evidence that no relationship exists.

## 3. Formal Proofs, Derivations, and Methods

### 3.1 Where the non-response formula comes from

The bias formula of Section 2.2 is not a rule to be memorised; it follows in three short steps from the definition of an average.

Step 1. Split the sample into the two groups that actually exist, the respondents and the non-respondents. Let the respondent share be $w_r$, the non-respondent share be $w_n$, the respondent mean be $\bar{x}_{r}$, and the non-respondent mean be $\bar{x}_{n}$. The two shares add to one, so $w_r + w_n = 1$.

Step 2. Write the mean of the whole sample as a weighted average of the two group means:

$$
\bar{x} = w_r \bar{x}_{r} + w_n \bar{x}_{n} .
$$

Step 3. Subtract the respondent mean from both sides and use $w_r = 1 - w_n$:

$$
\bar{x} - \bar{x}_{r} = \left(1 - w_n\right)\bar{x}_{r} + w_n \bar{x}_{n} - \bar{x}_{r} = w_n\left(\bar{x}_{n} - \bar{x}_{r}\right).
$$

The left-hand side is the distance between the true sample mean and the mean you would report if you used respondents only, so the right-hand side is exactly the bias. The derivation shows why the formula has two factors: one factor measures how much of the sample went missing, and the other measures how unusual the missing part was. Setting either factor to zero removes the bias.

### 3.2 Splitting an observed difference into effect and distortion

The confounding identity of Section 2.7 is derived by the same trick applied to two groups instead of two parts of one group.

Step 1. Let the two groups be a treated group and a comparison group, with means $\bar{y}_{1}$ and $\bar{y}_{0}$, and let the observed difference be $D = \bar{y}_{1} - \bar{y}_{0}$.

Step 2. Suppose the groups also differ in a second characteristic, and let the difference in that characteristic between the groups be $\Delta$, with the effect of one unit of that characteristic on the response equal to $b$.

Step 3. The part of the observed difference produced by the second characteristic is the product $b\Delta$, so the rest of the difference must be the treatment effect:

$$
D = \tau + b\Delta .
$$

Step 4. Solve for the treatment effect:

$$
\tau = D - b\Delta .
$$

The equation shows precisely what an observational study cannot do. The quantity $D$ is measured, but $\Delta$ is usually unknown because the researcher did not assign the groups, and $b$ must be estimated from somewhere else. Randomisation removes the difficulty at the root, because it makes $\Delta$ equal to zero in expectation, and then the observed difference is an honest estimate of the effect.

### 3.3 Why quadrupling the sample halves the error

Step 1. Start with the standard error of a mean of $n$ independent observations, which is the standard deviation of one observation divided by the square root of $n$:

$$
\text{SE}_n = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} .
$$

Step 2. Replace $n$ by four times $n$:

$$
\text{SE}_{4n} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{4n}} = \frac{\sigma}{2\sqrt{n}} .
$$

Step 3. Divide the second expression by the first:

$$
\frac{\text{SE}_{4n}}{\text{SE}_n} = \frac{1}{2} .
$$

So four times the work buys half the error. The same calculation in reverse says that to reduce the standard error by a factor of ten you must increase the sample by a factor of one hundred, which is why very precise surveys are expensive.

Step 4. Apply the same reasoning to a proportion. When a proportion $p$ is estimated from $n$ respondents, the standard error is

$$
\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{p\left(1-p\right)}{n}} ,
$$

where $p$ is the share answering in the category of interest and $1-p$ is the share answering otherwise. Here too the error falls only as the square root of the sample size.

### 3.4 Building a two-item reliability coefficient

Reliability can be computed from the variances of the items and the variance of the total.

Step 1. Consider two items with variances $\sigma_{1}^{2}$ and $\sigma_{2}^{2}$ and covariance $\sigma_{12}$. The variance of the sum of the two items is

$$
\sigma_{\text{total}}^{2} = \sigma_{1}^{2} + \sigma_{2}^{2} + 2\sigma_{12} .
$$

The covariance is the extent to which the two items move together; a positive covariance means respondents who score high on one item tend to score high on the other.

Step 2. Apply the general reliability formula for $k$ items, known as Cronbach's coefficient, which compares the sum of the item variances with the total variance:

$$
\alpha = \frac{k}{k-1}\left(1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{\text{total}}^{2}}\right).
$$

Here $k$ is the number of items, the sum in the numerator adds the variance of each item separately, and the denominator is the variance of the total score. The leading factor $k/(k-1)$ corrects for the fact that a longer test is mechanically more reliable.

Step 3. Read the formula as a statement about internal consistency. If the items vary independently of one another, the total variance is only the sum of the item variances, the fraction in brackets is zero, and the coefficient is zero. If the items move together perfectly, the total variance is much larger than the sum of the separate variances, the fraction in brackets approaches one, and the coefficient approaches one.

### 3.5 Method: designing a questionnaire in seven steps

Step 1. Fix the objective and the population in one sentence each, because a question that does not serve the objective should not be asked. Every item costs the respondent time and costs the survey response rate.

Step 2. Choose between open and closed questions item by item. An **open question** invites the respondent to answer in their own words and is used when the range of possible answers is unknown or when the reason behind an answer matters. A **closed question** offers a fixed list of options and is used when the categories are known in advance, because closed answers are quicker to give and far easier to tabulate.

Step 3. Write the wording plainly. Use short sentences, one idea per question, no jargon, no abbreviations, and no double negatives. Avoid **leading questions** that suggest a preferred answer, and avoid **double-barrelled questions** that ask two things at once, because the respondent can only give one reply to two issues.

Step 4. Order the questions deliberately. Begin with easy, non-threatening items so the respondent builds confidence, place sensitive items later when trust has been established, and group related items together. Earlier questions can influence later ones, so a question about satisfaction placed before a question about a specific fault will pull the fault answer towards the satisfaction answer.

Step 5. Pilot the instrument on a small group resembling the real respondents. A pilot of twenty or thirty people reveals ambiguous wording, missing response options, questions that everyone refuses, and the average time needed. Piloting is cheap; discovering a broken item after two thousand responses have been collected is not.

Step 6. Plan the administration to protect the response rate. Reminders, a clear statement of purpose, a guarantee of anonymity, a realistic length, and a choice of completion mode all raise the number of usable replies. The response rate is the share of eligible units that produced a usable record, and every design decision above moves it.

Step 7. Record the process, including the dates, the reminders, the number of undelivered instruments, and the number of refusals. These records are what allow the non-response bias of Section 2.2 to be assessed afterwards.

### 3.6 Method: designing an experiment with the four principles

Step 1. Apply **control**. Hold constant everything that is not the treatment, or compare against a control group that receives no treatment or a standard treatment, so that the comparison isolates the treatment.

Step 2. Apply **randomisation**. Assign units to treatments by a random mechanism, so that no characteristic of the units, measured or unmeasured, systematically favours one group.

Step 3. Apply **replication**. Apply each treatment to enough independent units that the average response of each group is precise, using the square-root law of Section 2.5 to judge how many are needed.

Step 4. Apply **blocking**. If some characteristic is known in advance to affect the response, group similar units into blocks and randomise within each block, so that the variation between blocks is removed before the treatments are compared.

The four principles answer four different threats. Control answers the threat of a competing explanation, randomisation answers the threat of systematic imbalance, replication answers the threat of random noise, and blocking answers the threat of wasted precision. An experiment missing any one of them is weaker in a specific and identifiable way.

### 3.7 Method: choosing the interview type

Step 1. Use a **structured interview** when the same fixed questions must be put to every respondent in the same order. It behaves like a spoken questionnaire, it standardises the encounter, and it produces answers that can be counted, at the cost of not being able to explore anything unexpected.

Step 2. Use a **semi-structured interview** when there is a fixed list of topics, called a guide, but the interviewer may reorder, probe, and follow up. It preserves comparability across respondents while allowing the interviewer to pursue a line of reasoning that the guide did not anticipate.

Step 3. Use an **unstructured interview** when the purpose is to discover what matters to the respondent rather than to measure a predetermined quantity. It resembles a guided conversation, it produces rich material, and it is very difficult to summarise across respondents or to repeat.

Step 4. Weigh the trade-off explicitly. Depth costs standardisation, and standardisation costs depth. State which of the two the research question needs, and choose the type that delivers it, rather than choosing the type that feels most thorough.

## 4. Representative Worked Examples and Solutions

### 4.1 Problem: the response rate of a postal survey

A researcher posts questionnaires to 600 households. Forty are returned as undelivered because the address no longer exists. Of the rest, 322 are returned completed. Compute the response rate.

### 4.2 Solution: remove the ineligible units first

Step 1. Identify the number of eligible units. The 40 undelivered questionnaires never reached a household, so they cannot count as refusals:

$$
n = 600 - 40 = 560 .
$$

Step 2. Identify the number of usable records, which is $r = 322$.

Step 3. Divide:

$$
\text{response rate} = \frac{322}{560} = 0.575 .
$$

Step 4. Interpret. The response rate is 57.5 per cent, so about four in every ten eligible households did not reply. Reporting 322 out of 600 would have given 53.7 per cent, which wrongly treats a wrong address as a refusal.

### 4.3 Problem: how much the non-respondents distort the average

The same survey asks how many hours per week the household spends shopping. The 322 respondents average 5.2 hours, and a follow-up of a random sample of the 238 non-respondents gives an average of 3.4 hours. Find the bias in the respondent average.

Step 1. Compute the respondent share and the non-respondent share:

$$
w_r = \frac{322}{560} = 0.575, \qquad w_n = \frac{238}{560} = 0.425 .
$$

Step 2. Compute the mean that would have been obtained from the whole sample:

$$
\bar{x} = 0.575\left(5.2\right) + 0.425\left(3.4\right) = 2.99 + 1.445 = 4.435 .
$$

Step 3. Subtract the respondent mean to obtain the bias:

$$
\text{bias} = 4.435 - 5.2 = -0.765 .
$$

Step 4. Interpret. The reported average of 5.2 hours overstates the truth by about 0.77 hours, because people who shop a lot were more willing to answer a question about shopping. The bias is negative when subtracted in this direction, and its size, not its sign, is what matters.

### 4.4 Problem: coverage error from an incomplete list

A town has 5 000 households. A sampling frame built from a telephone directory covers 4 200 of them; the remaining 800 have no listed telephone. Households in the frame spend an average of 950 shillings a week on vegetables, and households outside it spend an average of 640 shillings. Find the error introduced by using the frame.

Step 1. Write the frame mean and the true population mean. The frame mean is 950 shillings, and the true mean is the weighted average

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{4\,200\left(950\right) + 800\left(640\right)}{5\,000} = \frac{4\,502\,000}{5\,000} = 900.4 .
$$

Step 2. Subtract:

$$
\text{coverage error} = 950 - 900.4 = 49.6 .
$$

Step 3. Check by the short formula, which weights the gap by the omitted share:

$$
\frac{800}{5\,000}\left(950 - 640\right) = 0.16\left(310\right) = 49.6 .
$$

Step 4. Interpret. The frame overstates spending by about 50 shillings a week, and no increase in sample size removes the error, because the omitted households can never be selected however large the sample becomes.

### 4.5 Problem: an association that is partly confounding

In an observational study, 1 200 coffee drinkers and 800 non-drinkers are followed for five years. Heart disease occurs in 25 per cent of the coffee drinkers and 15 per cent of the non-drinkers. Among coffee drinkers 60 per cent smoke and among non-drinkers 20 per cent smoke. Within smokers the disease rate is 30 per cent for coffee drinkers and 25 per cent for non-drinkers; within non-smokers it is 17.5 per cent and 12.5 per cent. Separate the observed difference into effect and distortion.

Step 1. Record the observed difference:

$$
D = 25 - 15 = 10 \text{ percentage points} .
$$

Step 2. Record the difference in smoking prevalence:

$$
\Delta = 0.60 - 0.20 = 0.40 .
$$

Step 3. Record the effect of smoking on the disease rate inside the coffee group:

$$
b = 30 - 17.5 = 12.5 \text{ percentage points} .
$$

Step 4. Compute the distortion, which is the product of the two:

$$
C = b\Delta = 12.5\left(0.40\right) = 5 \text{ percentage points} .
$$

Step 5. Subtract to obtain the effect:

$$
\tau = D - C = 10 - 5 = 5 \text{ percentage points} .
$$

Step 6. Check by standardising both groups to the same smoking mix. With 44 per cent smokers overall, the standardised rates are

$$
0.44\left(30\right) + 0.56\left(17.5\right) = 23.0, \qquad 0.44\left(25\right) + 0.56\left(12.5\right) = 18.0 ,
$$

and the difference is $5$ percentage points, in agreement with Step 5.

Step 7. Interpret. Half the crude difference survives adjustment and half was produced by the fact that coffee drinkers were three times as likely to smoke. The adjusted difference is still observational, so it remains a candidate effect rather than a demonstrated cause.

### 4.6 Problem: a blocking comparison

Four shops each try a new display and the old display in successive weeks. The gains in sales, new minus old, are 3, 5, 7, and 5 units. The variance of a single week's sales is about 12 square units. Compare the precision of the blocked comparison with an unblocked comparison of two groups of four shops.

Step 1. Compute the mean gain:

$$
\bar{d} = \frac{3 + 5 + 7 + 5}{4} = 5 .
$$

Step 2. Compute the deviations and their squares: they are $-2$, $0$, $2$, $0$, with squares $4$, $0$, $4$, $0$, summing to $8$. With four observations and one estimated mean, the sample variance is

$$
s_d^{2} = \frac{8}{3} = 2.667 .
$$

Step 3. Compute the standard error of the blocked estimate:

$$
\text{SE}_{\text{blocked}} = \sqrt{\frac{2.667}{4}} = \sqrt{0.667} = 0.816 .
$$

Step 4. Compute the standard error of an unblocked comparison in which each group has four independent shops and the sales variance is 12:

$$
\text{SE}_{\text{unblocked}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\left(12\right)}{4}} = \sqrt{6} = 2.449 .
$$

Step 5. Compare by squaring the ratio of the two standard errors, which gives the relative efficiency:

$$
\frac{2.449^{2}}{0.816^{2}} = \frac{6}{0.667} = 9 .
$$

Step 6. Interpret. The blocked design is about nine times as efficient, because each shop acts as its own comparison and the large differences between shops drop out of the calculation.

### 4.7 Problem: repairing a bad questionnaire item

A draft item reads: "Do you not agree that the new bus timetable, which is an improvement, is convenient and reliable?" Rewrite it and say what was wrong.

Step 1. Identify the faults. The item is leading, because it announces that the timetable is an improvement. It is double-barrelled, because it asks about convenience and reliability at once. It is negative, because "do you not agree" forces the respondent to reverse the ordinary direction of agreement. It is long, because it embeds a claim inside the question.

Step 2. Rewrite as two closed items with balanced options. First: "How convenient is the new bus timetable?" with options very convenient, convenient, neither, inconvenient, very inconvenient. Second: "How reliable is the new bus timetable?" with the same five options.

Step 3. Consider whether an open item is better. If the objective is to discover what passengers dislike, an open item such as "What, if anything, would you change about the new bus timetable?" produces the reasons that closed options cannot anticipate.

Step 4. Note the design consequence. The rewrite multiplies the number of items, which lengthens the questionnaire and slightly lowers the response rate, so the gain in validity must be worth the cost in response.

### 4.8 Problem: reliability, and what it does to a correlation

A four-item scale has item variances 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, and 2.5, and the total score has variance 25.0. The scale is used to correlate attitude with behaviour, and the true correlation is believed to be 0.55. Find the reliability and the correlation that will actually be observed.

Step 1. Sum the item variances:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{4}\sigma_{i}^{2} = 2.0 + 2.5 + 3.0 + 2.5 = 10.0 .
$$

Step 2. Apply the coefficient with $k = 4$:

$$
\alpha = \frac{4}{3}\left(1 - \frac{10}{25}\right) = \frac{4}{3}\left(0.6\right) = 0.8 .
$$

Step 3. Apply the attenuation formula:

$$
r_{\text{obs}} = 0.55\sqrt{0.8} = 0.55\left(0.894\right) = 0.492 .
$$

Step 4. Interpret. A scale that is 80 per cent reliable reports a correlation of about 0.49 when the truth is 0.55. The loss looks small here, but a reliability of 0.5 would shrink 0.55 to 0.39, which many readers would dismiss as a weak association.

### 4.9 Problem: naming the method in five scenarios

For each scenario, name the method and the route.

| Scenario | Method | Route |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A clerk records how many customers enter a shop each hour from a doorway counter | Observational study | Observational |
| Two hundred passengers complete a printed sheet about waiting times | Questionnaire | Survey |
| One group of classes is taught with a new method, another with the old, assigned by coin toss | Experiment | Designed experiment |
| A manager reads last year's sales figures from a published annual report | Published source | Published source |
| A researcher sits with each of fifteen traders and asks about credit arrangements | Interview | Survey |

Step 1. Ask who controls the conditions. Only the third scenario involves deliberate assignment, so only that one is an experiment.

Step 2. Ask whether the researcher intervened at all. The first scenario records without intervening, so it is observational.

Step 3. Ask whether the answers were written and self-completed or spoken and recorded. The second is a questionnaire, and the fifth is an interview, although both belong to the survey route.

Step 4. Ask who collected the numbers. In the fourth scenario somebody else did, so the data are secondary.

## 5. Mathematical Extensions, Limits, and Common Errors

Beginners tend to treat the five methods as labels to be memorised, and then lose marks by attaching a label without justifying it. The justification is always the same two questions: who controlled the conditions, and how were the responses elicited? A scenario in which the researcher assigns the treatment is an experiment; a scenario in which the researcher only records what happens is an observational study; a scenario in which respondents answer questions is a survey, delivered either as a written questionnaire or as a spoken interview. Answering those two questions in the script, rather than writing a single word, is what converts a guess into an argued answer.

### 5.1 The mistakes that cost the most marks

- **Calling any study an experiment.** If the researcher did not assign the treatment, the study is observational, however controlled the recording was. Watching two groups that formed themselves is not an experiment.
- **Claiming causation from an observational study.** An association may be produced by a confounder, and the confounding identity of Section 2.7 shows that the observed difference cannot separate effect from distortion on its own.
- **Confusing a survey with a questionnaire.** The survey is the whole design, including the sampling and the administration; the questionnaire is one instrument used inside it. An interview schedule is also an instrument, and it is not a questionnaire.
- **Counting undelivered instruments as refusals.** The denominator of the response rate contains eligible units only, so wrong addresses, disconnected numbers, and deaths are removed before dividing.
- **Reporting a response rate without a denominator.** A bare statement that "322 questionnaires were returned" says nothing about representativeness until the number of eligible units is stated.
- **Assuming a high response rate guarantees a good survey.** A 95 per cent response rate from a badly covered frame is still biased, because the people outside the frame never had a chance to reply.
- **Writing leading or double-barrelled items.** Both are visible in a draft and both are cheap to repair, and neither can be repaired after the data are collected.
- **Skipping the pilot.** Piloting is the only stage at which ambiguity, missing options, and excessive length can be discovered while repair is still possible.
- **Treating reliability as validity.** A consistent instrument can be consistently wrong, which is exactly what an uncalibrated scale does.
- **Believing that a bigger sample fixes bias.** Precision improves with size; bias does not. A million observations from a distorted frame give a very confident wrong answer.

### 5.2 Limits of each method

A survey can describe a large population cheaply, but it depends entirely on self-report and on the willingness of respondents to answer honestly. An experiment can establish causation, but it can be unethical, expensive, or artificial, so its conclusions may not transfer to ordinary conditions. An observational study is realistic and often the only ethical option, but it cannot separate effect from confounding without strong additional assumptions. An interview yields depth and allows clarification, but it is slow, costly, and vulnerable to interviewer effects. A questionnaire reaches many respondents cheaply and standardises the questions, but it cannot probe an unexpected answer and it suffers most from non-response.

### 5.3 An extension: how large a sample is needed

Once the required precision is stated, the sample size follows from the standard error. For a proportion, the sample size needed to keep the margin of error at or below $e$ is approximately

$$
n = \frac{z^{2}p\left(1-p\right)}{e^{2}} ,
$$

where $z$ is the multiplier that fixes the confidence level, about $1.96$ for 95 per cent confidence; $p$ is the anticipated proportion; and $e$ is the permitted margin of error. The worst case is $p = 0.5$, because that value maximises the product $p(1-p)$. With $e = 0.04$ and $p = 0.5$,

$$
n = \frac{1.96^{2}\left(0.25\right)}{0.04^{2}} = \frac{0.9604}{0.0016} = 600.25 ,
$$

so 601 respondents are needed, and more must be approached to allow for non-response. If half the approached households fail to respond, the researcher must approach about 1 202 households to end with 601 responses, which is the practical reason response rate belongs in the planning stage and not only in the report.

### 5.4 A procedure for any question on this topic

1. State the population, the variable, and the sample in one sentence each, because every later claim refers to them.
2. Name the method and justify it by control of conditions and by mode of elicitation.
3. Decide whether the data are primary or secondary, and note what control is lost if they are secondary.
4. Identify the plausible confounders if the design is observational, and say what adjustment or design change would address them.
5. Compute the response rate with the correct denominator, then compute the non-response bias from the share and the gap.
6. Check the instrument for reliability and validity before trusting any correlation computed from it.
7. Attach a measure of reliability to every inference, and keep descriptive statements separate from inferential ones.

### 5.5 Consolidation

The topic reduces to a single chain. A question defines a population and a variable; a route is chosen from the four available routes; a method is chosen from the five named methods; an instrument is written, piloted, and administered; the responses become records; and the records support a descriptive summary and, if the design allows, an inference with a stated reliability. Randomisation is what licenses a causal sentence, replication is what buys precision, control is what removes competing explanations, and blocking is what stops a known nuisance from drowning the signal. Bias enters through coverage, non-response, and measurement, and none of those three is cured by collecting more data of the same kind.
