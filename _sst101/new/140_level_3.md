## 1. Foundational Concepts, Definitions, and Axioms

### 1.1 What is actually being tested here

This opening topic is examined almost entirely on precision rather than on computation. The questions that appear are of five recognisable kinds: recall a definition word for word; classify an activity as one of the five jobs that statistics performs; classify a variable by type and measurement scale; dissect a described study into population, variable, sample, inference, and measure of reliability; and apply the axioms of probability to a small equally likely sample space. A sixth kind, the short proof, asks you to deduce the complement rule or the addition law from the axioms.

Because the marks are attached to keywords, the strongest preparation is a keyword checklist rather than a set of worked drills. The mathematics in the topic is genuinely light, but the vocabulary is unforgiving: a definition that is ninety per cent right typically scores about half the available marks, and a probability answer that ignores an overlap scores nothing at all. The sections below therefore state each result in the form an examiner wants to see it, and flag the specific phrases that earn credit.

### 1.2 Statistics: the definition and its five verbs

> **Definition (Statistics).** Statistics is the science of collecting, organizing, presenting, analyzing, and interpreting data in order to support decisions under uncertainty.

The definition contains five verbs and one noun. A five-mark recall question is effectively one mark per verb, so an answer that says only "statistics is the study of data" surrenders four fifths of the available credit. Learn the verbs in the order given, because they also describe the order in which a study proceeds.

| Job | The activity that earns the mark | Typical examination wording |
|---|---|---|
| Collecting | Obtaining raw observations from a source | "A researcher administers a questionnaire to 200 households" |
| Organizing | Arranging observations into classes or ranks | "The responses are entered into a frequency table" |
| Presenting | Displaying organized data in tables or diagrams | "A histogram is drawn for the grouped data" |
| Analyzing | Computing summary measures and comparisons | "The mean and standard deviation are calculated" |
| Interpreting | Explaining what the analysis implies | "The report concludes that spending rises with income" |

Two distinctions are examined repeatedly. The first is organizing against presenting: counting into classes organizes, while drawing the picture presents. The second is analyzing against interpreting: computing a mean analyzes, while saying what the mean implies for policy interprets. If a question describes an activity and asks which job it represents, identify the object being acted upon, because counting into classes and drawing a chart both act on the same data but at different stages.

Statistics is divided into two branches that differ in intention rather than in technique.

> **Definition (Descriptive statistics).** Descriptive statistics uses numerical and graphical methods to look for patterns in a data set, and to present that information in a convenient form.

> **Definition (Inferential statistics).** Inferential statistics uses sample data to make estimates, decisions, predictions, or other generalizations about a larger set of data.

The discriminating test is the subject of the sentence. If the sentence is about the observations actually measured, it is descriptive. If it is about a group that was not fully measured, it is inferential, and it must be accompanied by a measure of reliability. A frequent examination trap is a sentence that quotes a sample mean and then quietly converts it into a claim about the population without stating any confidence; that answer loses the marking point for reliability even though the arithmetic is correct.

### 1.3 The five fundamental elements

> **Definition (Population).** A population is the set of all units, usually people, objects, transactions, or events, that we are interested in studying.

> **Definition (Variable).** A variable is a characteristic or property of an individual population unit.

> **Definition (Sample).** A sample is a subset of the units of a population.

> **Definition (Statistical inference).** A statistical inference is an estimate, prediction, or other generalization about a population based on information contained in a sample.

> **Definition (Measure of reliability).** A measure of reliability is a statement, attached to a statistical inference, that quantifies confidence in that inference.

Four points in these definitions are worth memorising as examination triggers. A population is defined by the question rather than by its size, so the same collection of people can be a population for one question and a sample for another. A variable belongs to a unit, so the population contains units and each unit carries values of the variable. A sample is a subset of units, not a subset of values. And a measure of reliability is a statement, not a number on its own, so the words "with ninety-five per cent confidence" or "plus or minus forty shillings" must be present in the answer.

When a question supplies a scenario and asks you to identify the elements, work through the definitions in the order in which they are listed. The population is the whole group the question is about. The variable is the characteristic measured on each unit, and it should be named together with its type and scale, because many mark schemes award a separate point for the classification. The sample is the group actually measured. The inference is the sentence that reaches beyond the sample. The measure of reliability is the phrase that limits the inference. Finally, state whether the study is descriptive or inferential, and justify the choice by pointing at the sentence that reaches beyond the data.

### 1.4 Types of data and the four measurement scales

> **Definition (Quantitative data).** Quantitative data are measurements recorded on a naturally occurring numerical scale.

> **Definition (Qualitative data).** Qualitative data are measurements that cannot be placed on a natural numerical scale and can only be classified into one of a group of categories.

The finer classification into four scales decides which arithmetic is legitimate, and examination questions regularly ask for both the broad type and the scale.

| Scale | Broad type | Defining property | Example | Legitimate operations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal | Qualitative | Categories with no order | Blood group, religion | Counting, mode |
| Ordinal | Qualitative | Ordered categories, unequal gaps | Grade A to E, rank | Counting, mode, median |
| Interval | Quantitative | Equal gaps, arbitrary zero | Temperature in Celsius | Addition and subtraction |
| Ratio | Quantitative | Equal gaps, true zero | Mass, income, time | All four operations |

Two further divisions are examined on the quantitative side. **Discrete** data take separate countable values, usually whole numbers, such as the number of children in a household. **Continuous** data take any value in an interval subject only to the precision of measurement, such as mass or waiting time. The division determines presentation: a discrete variable with few distinct values is tabulated value by value, while a continuous variable is grouped into classes.

Three errors recur in this area. Coding a qualitative category with a digit does not make the data quantitative, because the digit is a label and the average of two labels is meaningless. Interval data cannot be used to make ratio statements, so it is wrong to say that twenty degrees is twice as warm as ten degrees. Ordinal data cannot be averaged, because the distances between adjacent grades are not equal.

### 1.5 Sources of data and the strength of each

Data are obtained in four ways, and each route supports a different strength of conclusion.

- **A published source** supplies figures gathered by somebody else. It is cheap and often large, but the questions it can answer were fixed by whoever collected it.
- **A designed experiment** is one in which the investigator assigns the treatments. Because the assignment is controlled, this is the only route that supports a causal conclusion.
- **A survey** collects responses from a chosen group. It is efficient for opinions and self-reported facts, but vulnerable to non-response and to socially desirable answering.
- **Observational collection** records what happens without interference. It reflects real behaviour but cannot separate the variable of interest from other factors that move with it.

The examination point is the causal claim. A study that compares learners who eat breakfast with learners who do not is observational, so the difference in marks is an association that may be explained by any of the uncontrolled differences between the two groups. A study that randomly assigns half a group to breakfast and half to no breakfast is a designed experiment, and only then may the difference be attributed to the treatment.

### 1.6 Frequency distributions and their vocabulary

A **frequency distribution** is a table in which the data are grouped into classes and the number of cases falling in each class is recorded. That count is the **frequency** of the class.

A **discrete frequency distribution** lists each distinct value with its frequency. A **grouped frequency distribution** replaces single values by classes, and the vocabulary of classes must be exact.

- A **class interval** is the symbol defining a class, for example from ten to nineteen.
- The two numbers in the symbol are the **class limits**; the left-hand number is the **lower class limit** and the right-hand number the **upper class limit**.
- A **class boundary**, also called a true class limit, is the true dividing line between adjacent classes, placed halfway across the gap between them, so the classes ten to nineteen and twenty to twenty-nine are divided at nineteen point five.
- The **class width**, or class size, is the difference between the upper boundary and the lower boundary of a class.
- The **class mark** is the midpoint of a class, computed as the average of its two boundaries.
- The **cumulative frequency** up to a class is the total of all frequencies at or below that class.
- The **relative frequency** of a class is its frequency divided by the total number of observations.

Three formulas follow immediately, and each is worth a mark on its own.

$$
h = U - L
$$

In that expression h is the class width, U is the upper boundary of the class, and L is the lower boundary. The width is never computed from the limits when the gap between adjacent classes is not one unit, because the limits omit half of the gap at each end.

$$
m = \frac{L + U}{2}
$$

Here m is the class mark, and L and U are again the boundaries of the same class. The class mark is the value that represents every observation in the class when a grouped summary is computed.

$$
F_j = \sum_{i=1}^{j} f_i
$$

In this expression F with a subscript j is the cumulative frequency up to class j, f with a subscript i is the frequency of class i, and the sigma sign adds the frequencies from the first class up to class j.

### 1.7 Probability: the primitives

> **Definition (Probability).** Probability is the study of uncertainty and of the likelihood of events.

The three primitives are used in every probability question, and confusion between the second and third is the most common single error in the topic.

- An **experiment** is any process whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty in advance.
- The **sample space**, written S, is the set of all possible outcomes of the experiment.
- An **event** is a subset of the sample space; it occurs when the outcome of the experiment lies inside it.
- Two events are **mutually exclusive** when they cannot occur together, so their intersection is empty and the probability of both is zero.
- A collection of events is **exhaustive** when their union is the whole sample space, so at least one of them must occur.
- Outcomes are **equally likely** when the model assigns each of them the same probability; the classical definition of probability requires this.

For a finite sample space with equally likely outcomes, the probability of an event is the ratio of the number of outcomes it contains to the number in the whole space.

$$
P(A) = \frac{n(A)}{n(S)}
$$

Here P of A is the probability of the event A, the symbol n of A is the number of outcomes in A, and n of S is the number of outcomes in the sample space. The formula is valid only when the outcomes are equally likely, which is an assumption and should be stated in an answer that uses it.

### 1.8 The three axioms and the results they generate

> **Axiom 1 (Non-negativity).** For every event A, the probability of A is at least zero.

> **Axiom 2 (Normalisation).** The probability of the whole sample space is one.

> **Axiom 3 (Countable additivity).** If events are mutually exclusive, the probability of their union is the sum of their probabilities.

Everything else is a theorem. The complement rule, the addition law for two events, the addition law for three events, monotonicity, and Boole's inequality are all deductions, and a proof question that begins "using the axioms of probability" expects you to start from these three statements and nothing else. Writing down the classical formula as though it were an axiom is a common way to lose the first mark of a proof question.

### 1.9 The notation sheet

Learn the symbols before the results, because a formula misread is a formula misapplied.

| Symbol | Meaning | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| n | Number of observations in the sample | Every summary measure |
| x with subscript i | The i-th observation | Summation formulas |
| x bar | Arithmetic mean of the sample | Mean, variance, standard error |
| f with subscript i | Frequency of the i-th class | Grouped tables |
| m with subscript j | Class mark of the j-th class | Grouped mean |
| h | Class width | Grouped tables, coding |
| F with subscript j | Cumulative frequency up to class j | Ogive, median class |
| S | Sample space | Every probability question |
| A, B, C | Events, that is subsets of S | Probability laws |
| A with a dash | Complement of A | Complement rule |
| P of A | Probability of event A | Every probability statement |
| The cup symbol | Union, read as "or" | Addition law |
| The cap symbol | Intersection, read as "and" | Addition law, independence |

The single most damaging notation error in the topic is the confusion between the sum of the squares and the square of the sum. The quantity in which each value is squared and then the squares are added is a different number from the quantity in which the values are added and then the total is squared, and the two agree only in trivial cases. Every formula involving squared deviations uses the first, so an answer that squares the total is wrong from that point onward.

## 2. Theorems, Lemmas, and Analytical Properties

Each result below is stated in the form it should be quoted, followed by the one line that explains why it matters in an examination. Where a result is used inside a longer proof, the mark scheme normally awards a point for naming it.

### 2.1 Summation algebra

> **Lemma (Linearity of the summation operator).** For constants a and b, and for two sequences of equal length,

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(a x_i + b y_i\right) = a\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i + b\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i
$$

Two corollaries are used so often that they are worth quoting as separate facts.

> **Corollary (Sum of a constant).** A constant c taken n times sums to n times c.

> **Corollary (Scaling).** Multiplying every observation by a constant multiplies the total, and therefore the mean, by the same constant.

The examination use is almost always a shortcut. Any question that gives a table of large class marks can be reduced to a table of small integers by subtracting a convenient constant and dividing by the class width, and the linearity lemma is the justification for that reduction. If a mark scheme asks you to "justify the coding", this is the result to name.

### 2.2 Identities for the arithmetic mean

Let the mean of the observations be the total divided by the count.

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i
$$

> **Theorem (Zero sum of deviations).** The deviations of the observations from their own mean sum to zero.

> **Theorem (Shift of origin).** Subtracting a constant A from every observation lowers the mean by exactly A, so the mean can be recovered by adding A back.

> **Theorem (Weighted mean).** When values repeat with frequencies, the mean of all the observations equals the sum of value times frequency, divided by the total frequency.

> **Theorem (Minimum sum of squares).** Among all constants, the sum of squared deviations is smallest when the constant is the mean.

The third theorem is the one that turns a frequency table into a mean, and the first is the free arithmetic check that a candidate should apply to every computed mean. The fourth theorem explains why the mean rather than the median appears in the definitions of variance, standard deviation, and the least-squares line, which are examined in later topics.

### 2.3 Identities for grouped data

> **Lemma (Marks advance by the width).** When all classes have equal width h and the first class mark is m with subscript one, the class marks form an arithmetic progression whose common difference is h.

> **Theorem (Grouped mean).** If every observation in a class is replaced by that class's mark, the resulting mean is the weighted average of the class marks, weighted by the class frequencies.

> **Theorem (Monotonicity of cumulative frequency).** The cumulative frequency never decreases from class to class, and the final cumulative frequency equals the total number of observations.

> **Theorem (Grouping error bound).** The grouped mean differs from the true mean by at most half a class width.

The third result is the structural check that protects every grouped table, and the fourth is the sentence that should accompany any reported grouped mean. Stating the bound converts an approximation into a defensible statement, and examiners award a mark for it when the question says "comment on the accuracy of your answer".

### 2.4 Theorems of probability

> **Theorem (Probability of the empty event).** An event containing no outcomes has probability zero.

> **Theorem (Complement rule).** For any event A with complement A dash,

$$
P\left(A'\right) = 1 - P(A)
$$

> **Theorem (Monotonicity).** If every outcome of A is also an outcome of B, then the probability of A is at most the probability of B.

> **Theorem (Addition law for two events).** For any two events A and B,

$$
P\left(A \cup B\right) = P(A) + P(B) - P\left(A \cap B\right)
$$

> **Theorem (Addition law for three events).** For any three events A, B, and C,

$$
P\left(A \cup B \cup C\right) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P\left(A \cap B\right) - P\left(A \cap C\right) - P\left(B \cap C\right) + P\left(A \cap B \cap C\right)
$$

> **Theorem (Boole's inequality).** For any finite collection of events, the probability of the union is at most the sum of the individual probabilities.

> **Theorem (Law of total probability).** If the events A subscript one to A subscript k are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, then for any event B,

$$
P(B) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P\left(B \mid A_i\right) P\left(A_i\right)
$$

The pattern in the two addition laws is alternation: signs alternate as the intersections get larger, and the last term always has the sign appropriate to the number of sets. This is the inclusion and exclusion principle, and a candidate who remembers only the two-event law can always reach the three-event law by applying the two-event law twice, which is exactly what Section 3.3 does.

The conditional probability written with a vertical bar in the law of total probability is the probability of B given that A subscript i has occurred. It is not examined as a computation in this topic, but the law is the step immediately preceding Bayes' theorem, and it is the correct answer to any question that asks how a partitioned sample space is handled.

### 2.5 Properties that govern inference

> **Property (Sampling error is unavoidable).** Any summary computed from a sample differs from the corresponding population summary, and no formula applied to the sample alone can reveal the size of that difference.

> **Property (Precision improves with sample size).** Other things being equal, a larger sample supports a narrower interval of plausible values.

> **Property (Accuracy is not precision).** A large sample drawn by a biased method can produce a narrow interval around a systematically wrong value.

This trio is the standard answer to any "comment on the reliability" question. It also supplies the reason a measure of reliability must name the sampling method as well as the confidence level: the interval describes the behaviour of a procedure, and a procedure that selects a non-representative subset produces intervals that are consistently displaced.

### 2.6 A high-yield summary of the results

| Result | Statement in one line | Typical mark value |
|---|---|---|
| Complement rule | Probability of not A is one minus probability of A | Two |
| Two-event addition law | Union equals sum minus intersection | Three |
| Three-event addition law | Alternating sum over all intersections | Four |
| Weighted mean | Sum of value times frequency over total frequency | Two |
| Grouped mean | Weighted mean of class marks | Three |
| Class width | Upper boundary minus lower boundary | One |
| Class mark | Average of the two boundaries | One |
| Relative frequency | Frequency divided by total frequency | One |
| Grouping error bound | At most half a class width | Two |
| Boole's inequality | Union is at most the sum of the parts | Two |

## 3. Formal Proofs, Derivations, and Methods

Proof questions in this topic are short and heavily marked per step. The two that recur are the derivation of the complement rule and the derivation of the addition law, and both must begin from the axioms rather than from the classical formula.

### 3.1 Deriving the complement rule from the axioms

**Claim.** For any event A inside a sample space S, the probability of the complement of A is one minus the probability of A.

Step 1. State the two set facts. The event A and its complement A dash have no outcome in common, so their intersection is empty, and together they cover the sample space, so their union is S. *One mark for both set facts.*

Step 2. Apply the third axiom, additivity, to the two mutually exclusive events.

$$
P(A) + P\left(A'\right) = P\left(A \cup A'\right)
$$

*One mark for invoking additivity correctly.*

Step 3. Replace the union by the sample space, using the second set fact.

$$
P\left(A \cup A'\right) = P(S)
$$

*One mark for the substitution.*

Step 4. Apply the second axiom, normalisation, to obtain a numerical value.

$$
P(S) = 1
$$

*One mark for the value.*

Step 5. Combine the three displayed statements and rearrange.

$$
P\left(A'\right) = 1 - P(A)
$$

*One mark for the correct rearrangement and one for stating the conclusion.*

Two remarks earn credit in a well-written answer. First, if the probability of A were allowed to exceed one, the rule would force a negative probability for the complement, which the first axiom forbids; this is why the rule is consistent with the axioms rather than merely following from them. Second, no assumption of equally likely outcomes was used anywhere in the derivation, so the rule holds for every probability model, including those built from relative frequencies.

### 3.2 Deriving the addition law for two events

**Claim.** For any two events A and B, the probability of their union equals the sum of their probabilities minus the probability of their intersection.

Step 1. Split the union into two pieces that do not overlap: everything in A, together with everything in B that lies outside A.

$$
A \cup B = A \cup \left(B \cap A'\right)
$$

*One mark for a valid disjoint decomposition.*

Step 2. Apply additivity to the two disjoint pieces.

$$
P\left(A \cup B\right) = P(A) + P\left(B \cap A'\right)
$$

*One mark.*

Step 3. Split B in the same way, into the part inside A and the part outside A, and apply additivity again.

$$
P(B) = P\left(A \cap B\right) + P\left(B \cap A'\right)
$$

*Two marks, one for the decomposition of B and one for the additivity step.*

Step 4. Rearrange the third step to isolate the probability of the part of B outside A.

$$
P\left(B \cap A'\right) = P(B) - P\left(A \cap B\right)
$$

*One mark.*

Step 5. Substitute that expression into the second step.

$$
P\left(A \cup B\right) = P(A) + P(B) - P\left(A \cap B\right)
$$

*Two marks, one for the substitution and one for the final statement.*

The logic of the proof is worth stating in words, because a candidate who can say it can reconstruct it under pressure. The union is A plus the part of B that A does not already contain. The whole of B is the overlap plus that same outside part. Subtracting the overlap therefore converts the sum of the two probabilities into the probability of the union, because it removes the piece that was counted twice.

### 3.3 Extending to three events by two applications

Apply the two-event law to the union of A and B, treating that union as a single event.

$$
P\left(A \cup B \cup C\right) = P\left(A \cup B\right) + P(C) - P\left(\left(A \cup B\right) \cap C\right)
$$

Distribute the intersection over the union in the last term.

$$
\left(A \cup B\right) \cap C = \left(A \cap C\right) \cup \left(B \cap C\right)
$$

Apply the two-event law to that union.

$$
P\left(\left(A \cap C\right) \cup \left(B \cap C\right)\right) = P\left(A \cap C\right) + P\left(B \cap C\right) - P\left(A \cap B \cap C\right)
$$

Substitute both pieces and collect the terms.

$$
P\left(A \cup B \cup C\right) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P\left(A \cap B\right) - P\left(A \cap C\right) - P\left(B \cap C\right) + P\left(A \cap B \cap C\right)
$$

The marks for this derivation are awarded for the correct distribution of the intersection, for the second application of the two-event law, and for the sign of the triple intersection term. Candidates who memorise the formula but cannot derive it usually lose the sign of the last term, and the loss is complete when the question supplies a numerical table in which the triple intersection is non-zero.

### 3.4 The grouped mean and the size of its error

Let the observations in class j have frequencies f subscript j and class marks m subscript j. Replacing every observation by its class mark gives the weighted mean of the marks.

$$
\bar{x}_{\text{grouped}} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k} f_j m_j}{\sum_{j=1}^{k} f_j}
$$

Now let the true mean of the observations inside class j be mu subscript j. The difference between the grouped mean and the true mean is a weighted average of the differences between the marks and the within-class means.

$$
\bar{x}_{\text{grouped}} - \bar{x}_{\text{true}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k} f_j\left(m_j - \mu_j\right)
$$

Because the class mark is the midpoint of the class, every observation in the class lies within half a class width of it, so each difference on the right is bounded in size by half the class width. The weighted average of quantities that are each bounded by half the width is itself bounded by half the width.

$$
\left|\bar{x}_{\text{grouped}} - \bar{x}_{\text{true}}\right| \le \frac{h}{2}
$$

The bound is the standard answer to "state the accuracy of the grouped mean". It also tells you how to improve the estimate: halve the class width and the worst-case error halves with it. The bound is worst-case, so a real error is usually smaller, but a candidate who quotes the bound cannot be accused of overstating the reliability of the approximation.

### 3.5 Method: constructing a grouped frequency distribution

1. Find the smallest and largest observations and subtract to obtain the range.
2. Choose the number of classes. Sturges' rule gives a starting value of one plus three point three two two times the base ten logarithm of n, rounded up.
3. Divide the range by the number of classes and round the result up to a convenient width such as five, ten, or twenty.
4. Place the lower boundary of the first class at or just below the minimum, then generate successive classes by adding the width.
5. Tally each observation into exactly one class, placing a value that sits on a boundary into the class whose lower boundary it equals.
6. Add the frequencies and check that they total n. Add the cumulative frequencies and check that the last entry equals n.
7. Add the relative frequencies and check that they total one.

Steps six and seven are where the marks for "correct table" are protected. A table that totals incorrectly usually contains a single misplaced observation, and finding it takes seconds if the total is written down and minutes if it is not.

### 3.6 Method: the coding route to a grouped mean

1. Compute the class marks from the boundaries, not from the limits.
2. Choose an assumed mean A, normally the mark of a class near the middle of the table.
3. Choose the coding unit h, normally the common class width.
4. Form the coded values by subtracting A from each mark and dividing by h, giving small integers.
5. Multiply each coded value by its class frequency, add the products, and divide by the total frequency to obtain the mean of the coded values.
6. Transform back using the coding identity, which adds A and adds h times the mean of the coded values.

The identity that justifies step six is derived from linearity alone, and it is worth writing down once so that it can be quoted.

$$
\bar{x} = A + h\bar{u}
$$

Here A is the assumed mean, h is the coding unit, u bar is the mean of the coded values, and x bar is the required mean. The method exists because multiplying small integers by small frequencies is far less error-prone than multiplying four-digit marks by two-digit frequencies, and because an arithmetic slip in a coded table is usually obvious from the size of the numbers.

### 3.7 Method: probabilities of "at least one"

1. Write the event in the exact words of the question, and note whether it is phrased as a negation or as a threshold.
2. Identify the complement, which for "at least one" is "none at all".
3. Compute the probability of the complement by counting its outcomes, or by multiplying when the trials are independent.
4. Subtract the result from one, because the two events are complementary.
5. Check that the answer is at least as large as the probability of any single component event, since a union cannot be less likely than one of its parts.

The fifth step is a sanity check that catches the commonest error in this family of questions, which is subtracting in the wrong direction and thereby producing a probability smaller than the event being contained in the union.

## 4. Representative Worked Examples and Solutions

The four solutions below are written as they should be written in an answer booklet: numbered steps, one algebraic stage per line, and a closing sentence that says what the answer means. The bracketed notes show where the marks sit; they are not part of a submitted answer.

### 4.1 Problem: a full grouped-data question, twelve marks

The daily takings of forty market traders, recorded in hundreds of shillings, are grouped as follows: the class ten to nineteen has frequency six, the class twenty to twenty-nine has frequency thirteen, the class thirty to thirty-nine has frequency thirteen, and the class forty to forty-nine has frequency eight.

(a) State the class boundaries and the class width. (b) Compute the class marks. (c) Estimate the mean by the coding method. (d) Identify the median class and estimate the median. (e) Comment on the accuracy of the estimate in part (c).

### 4.2 Solution: boundaries, marks, coding, median, accuracy

Step 1. Note that consecutive limits differ by one unit, so half of that gap is one half, and the boundaries lie one half beyond each limit.

$$
9.5, \quad 19.5, \quad 29.5, \quad 39.5, \quad 49.5
$$

*Two marks, one for the boundaries and one for the width stated next.*

Step 2. Subtract the lower boundary from the upper boundary of any class to obtain the width.

$$
h = 19.5 - 9.5 = 10
$$

Step 3. Average the boundaries of each class to obtain its mark.

$$
m_1 = 14.5, \quad m_2 = 24.5, \quad m_3 = 34.5, \quad m_4 = 44.5
$$

*Two marks, one for the method and one for all four values correct.*

Step 4. Choose the assumed mean and the coding unit, taking the third class mark and the common width.

$$
A = 34.5, \qquad h = 10
$$

Step 5. Convert every mark to a coded value, subtract the assumed mean and divide by the width, and record the frequencies beside them.

| Class | Mark | Frequency | Coded value | Frequency times coded value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 to 19 | 14.5 | 6 | -2 | -12 |
| 20 to 29 | 24.5 | 13 | -1 | -13 |
| 30 to 39 | 34.5 | 13 | 0 | 0 |
| 40 to 49 | 44.5 | 8 | 1 | 8 |
| Total | not applicable | 40 | not applicable | -17 |

*Two marks for the coded column and one for the weighted total.*

Step 6. Divide the weighted total of the coded values by the total frequency.

$$
\bar{u} = \frac{-17}{40} = -0.425
$$

Step 7. Transform back with the coding identity.

$$
\bar{x} = A + h\bar{u} = 34.5 + 10(-0.425) = 34.5 - 4.25 = 30.25
$$

*Three marks, one for the identity, one for the substitution, and one for the correct value.*

Step 8. Verify by the direct route, which is a legitimate use of examination time when the coded answer is untidy.

$$
\frac{6(14.5) + 13(24.5) + 13(34.5) + 8(44.5)}{40} = \frac{87 + 318.5 + 448.5 + 356}{40} = \frac{1210}{40} = 30.25
$$

Step 9. Locate the median class. Half of forty is twenty, and the cumulative frequencies are six, nineteen, thirty-two, and forty, so the twentieth observation lies in the class thirty to thirty-nine.

*One mark for identifying the correct class.*

Step 10. Estimate the median by linear interpolation inside that class, adding to the lower boundary the fraction of the class needed to reach the twentieth observation.

$$
\text{median} \approx 29.5 + \frac{20 - 19}{13}\left(10\right) = 29.5 + 0.77 = 30.27
$$

*One mark for the interpolation formula and one for the value.*

Step 11. Comment on accuracy. Because every observation was replaced by its class mark, the computed mean differs from the true mean by at most half a class width, which is five hundred shillings here.

*Two marks, one for naming the cause of the error and one for the numerical bound.*

Step 12. Interpret. The average daily taking is about three thousand and twenty-five shillings, and the approximation is accurate to within about five hundred shillings in the worst case; halving the class width would halve that bound.

### 4.3 Problem: a probability question with overlap, ten marks

Cards numbered from one to fifteen are placed in a box and one card is drawn at random. Let A be the event that the number is even and B the event that the number is a multiple of three. Find the probability of A, the probability of B, the probability that the number is even or a multiple of three, and the probability that it is neither.

### 4.4 Solution: count the events, then apply the laws

Step 1. State the sample space and confirm that its outcomes are equally likely.

$$
S = \{1, 2, 3, \ldots, 15\}, \qquad n(S) = 15
$$

*One mark for a correct sample space with its size.*

Step 2. Write A as a set and count it.

$$
A = \{2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14\}, \qquad P(A) = \frac{7}{15}
$$

*Two marks, one for the set and one for the probability.*

Step 3. Write B as a set and count it.

$$
B = \{3, 6, 9, 12, 15\}, \qquad P(B) = \frac{5}{15}
$$

*Two marks.*

Step 4. Identify the intersection, which is the set of numbers that are both even and multiples of three, and note that it is not empty.

$$
A \cap B = \{6, 12\}, \qquad P\left(A \cap B\right) = \frac{2}{15}
$$

*Two marks, one for recognising that the events overlap and one for the correct probability.*

Step 5. Apply the addition law.

$$
P\left(A \cup B\right) = \frac{7}{15} + \frac{5}{15} - \frac{2}{15} = \frac{10}{15} = \frac{2}{3}
$$

*Two marks, one for the law and one for the value in lowest terms.*

Step 6. Apply the complement rule for the final part.

$$
P\left(\left(A \cup B\right)'\right) = 1 - \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{3}
$$

*One mark.*

Step 7. Verify by direct enumeration, which is quick and protects the whole answer.

$$
A \cup B = \{2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15\}
$$

That set contains ten numbers, and the five numbers left out are one, five, seven, eleven, and thirteen, which is one third of fifteen. Both answers are therefore confirmed.

### 4.5 Problem: dissecting a described study, eight marks

A registrar at a college with four thousand eight hundred students wishes to know the average monthly amount students spend on mobile data. She selects two hundred students at random, records their spending, obtains a mean of one thousand two hundred and fifty shillings, and reports that the average for all students lies between one thousand one hundred and eighty and one thousand three hundred and twenty shillings, with ninety-five per cent confidence. Identify the population, the variable and its type, the sample, the inference, and the measure of reliability. State, with a reason, whether the study is descriptive or inferential.

### 4.6 Solution: match each phrase to its definition

Step 1. Name the population. The population is the set of all four thousand eight hundred students at the college, because that is the group the question is about.

*One mark.*

Step 2. Name the variable and classify it. The variable is the monthly amount spent on mobile data; it is quantitative, measured on a ratio scale, because a spend of zero is a genuine absence of spending.

*Two marks, one for the variable and one for the classification.*

Step 3. Name the sample. The sample is the two hundred students who were selected and measured.

*One mark.*

Step 4. Name the inference. The inference is the statement that the average for all students lies between one thousand one hundred and eighty and one thousand three hundred and twenty shillings, because that statement reaches beyond the students measured.

*Two marks, one for identifying the interval and one for explaining that it concerns the population.*

Step 5. Name the measure of reliability. The measure of reliability is the phrase "with ninety-five per cent confidence", which states how often the procedure produces an interval containing the true average.

*One mark.*

Step 6. Classify the study and justify. The study is inferential, because the interval is a generalisation about a population based on a sample; the mean of one thousand two hundred and fifty shillings is the descriptive summary on which that generalisation rests.

*One mark.*

### 4.7 Problem: a frequency distribution and its mean, six marks

Twenty learners reported the number of textbooks they own: one, three, two, two, zero, four, one, two, five, three, two, one, four, two, zero, three, two, one, four, two. Construct a frequency distribution with relative frequencies, and compute the mean.

### 4.8 Solution: tally, tabulate, total, divide

Step 1. Note the range, which runs from zero to five.

Step 2. Count each value in a single pass: zero occurs twice, one four times, two seven times, three three times, four three times, and five once.

Step 3. Tabulate the counts with relative frequencies.

| Textbooks | Frequency | Relative frequency |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 2 | 0.10 |
| 1 | 4 | 0.20 |
| 2 | 7 | 0.35 |
| 3 | 3 | 0.15 |
| 4 | 3 | 0.15 |
| 5 | 1 | 0.05 |
| Total | 20 | 1.00 |

*Three marks, one for the frequencies, one for the relative frequencies, and one for both totals.*

Step 4. Compute the weighted total.

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{6} f_i x_i = 2(0) + 4(1) + 7(2) + 3(3) + 3(4) + 1(5) = 0 + 4 + 14 + 9 + 12 + 5 = 44
$$

*Two marks.*

Step 5. Divide by the total frequency.

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{44}{20} = 2.2
$$

*One mark.*

Step 6. Interpret. On average each learner owns two point two textbooks, and the modal holding is two, reported by seven learners, which is thirty-five per cent of the group.

## 5. Mathematical Extensions, Limits, and Common Errors

### 5.1 The mark-losing errors, and the correction for each

1. **Defining statistics without its verbs.** A bare "study of data" answer forfeits most of the marks. Write collecting, organizing, presenting, analyzing, and interpreting.
2. **Treating the sample mean as a population claim.** State whether the sentence is descriptive or inferential, and attach the confidence phrase whenever it is inferential.
3. **Omitting the measure of reliability.** Any inference without one is an unsupported assertion and usually loses a specific mark.
4. **Coding qualitative data and calling it quantitative.** A digit assigned to a category is a label, not a measurement.
5. **Averaging ordinal data.** Grades and ranks may be ordered but their gaps are not equal, so their mean has no meaning.
6. **Using class limits where boundaries are required.** The width and the mark must both be computed from boundaries.
7. **Computing the width as upper limit minus lower limit.** With a one-unit gap this understates the width by one unit and shifts every subsequent figure.
8. **Leaving out the total row.** Without it, a tallying error survives unchecked into the mean.
9. **Adding probabilities of overlapping events.** The intersection must be subtracted once, and the three-event case needs the alternating signs.
10. **Confusing mutually exclusive with independent.** Mutually exclusive events with positive probability are strongly dependent, not independent.
11. **Calling the sample space an event.** The sample space lists every outcome; an event is a chosen subset of it.
12. **Squaring the total instead of the values.** The square of a sum and the sum of squares are different quantities, and the mistake corrupts every formula built on squared deviations.

### 5.2 The limits of the methods taught here

The grouped mean is an approximation whose worst error is half a class width, so it should never be reported as though it were exact. The accuracy improves as the class width shrinks, but the table then becomes longer and its classes sparser, so the choice of width is a compromise rather than a calculation. Two tables built from the same data with different widths will give slightly different means, and both are legitimate provided the width is stated.

A probability computed from observed data is also an estimate. Relative frequencies settle down as the number of repetitions grows, but they never become exact, and two samples of the same size from the same population will give slightly different estimates. The axioms still constrain the estimate, so it must lie between zero and one, and the estimates of mutually exclusive and exhaustive events must still sum to one; those constraints are the checks available without further theory.

Finally, the whole inferential apparatus here is a promise rather than a demonstration. The topic asserts that a sample supports a population claim with a stated reliability, but it does not derive the sampling distributions that make such statements quantitative. Those come later, and a candidate should not attempt to fabricate a confidence interval in this topic; describing the structure correctly is what is being examined.

### 5.3 Extensions that examiners like

Boole's inequality is the natural extension of additivity to events that are not mutually exclusive, and it is often asked as a two-mark "show that" question. Because the union is at most the sum of the individual probabilities, a quick upper bound for a union can be written down without knowing any intersections.

The law of total probability generalises the addition law to a partition of the sample space. When the events A subscript one to A subscript k split the space into non-overlapping pieces, the probability of any event B is the sum over the pieces of the conditional probability of B given the piece, multiplied by the probability of the piece. This is the step immediately preceding Bayes' theorem, and it is worth recognising even at this stage.

Chebyshev's inequality supplies the first distribution-free statement about spread. For any distribution with mean mu and finite standard deviation sigma, the probability that an observation lies at least k standard deviations from the mean is at most one over k squared. With k equal to two, at least three quarters of the observations lie within two standard deviations of the mean, whatever the shape of the distribution. That result is the reliability statement available before any distributional assumption is made, and it is the honest answer to "what can be said without assuming normality".

### 5.4 Revision checklist

- I can state the definition of statistics with all five verbs, and classify any described activity as one of them.
- I can define descriptive and inferential statistics and discriminate between them in a written scenario.
- I can define population, variable, sample, statistical inference, and measure of reliability, and identify all five in a paragraph of description.
- I can classify a variable as qualitative or quantitative and place it on the nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio scale, with a reason.
- I can name the four sources of data and say which of them supports a causal conclusion.
- I can define class interval, limits, boundaries, width, mark, cumulative frequency, and relative frequency, and compute all of them from a table.
- I can state the three axioms of probability from memory and mark which standard results are theorems rather than axioms.
- I can derive the complement rule and the two-event addition law from the axioms, in numbered steps.
- I can compute a grouped mean by the direct route and by the coding route and show that the two agree.
- I can state the half-width bound on the error of a grouped mean and use it to comment on accuracy.
- I can compute a probability on a small equally likely sample space, handling overlap and negation correctly.
- I can explain why precision is not accuracy and why the sampling method limits the reliability statement.

### 5.5 Time strategy in the examination

| Question type | Typical marks | Plan | Write | Check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Definition recall | 4 to 6 | 1 minute | 4 minutes | 1 minute |
| Classification of activities or data | 4 | 1 minute | 3 minutes | 1 minute |
| Frequency table construction | 8 to 10 | 2 minutes | 6 minutes | 2 minutes |
| Grouped mean with interpretation | 10 to 12 | 3 minutes | 8 minutes | 2 minutes |
| Probability with overlap or negation | 8 to 10 | 2 minutes | 6 minutes | 2 minutes |
| Short proof from the axioms | 5 to 8 | 2 minutes | 5 minutes | 1 minute |

Work in order of certainty rather than order of appearance. Begin with the definition and classification questions, which take a few minutes and secure marks that cannot be lost later, then move to the computational questions while the mind is fresh, and leave the proof until last unless it is worth more than the computations. Reserve the final five minutes for the checks: frequencies total n, relative frequencies total one, encoded values sum correctly, and every quoted probability lies between zero and one.

### 5.6 What a full-mark answer looks like

A full-mark answer in this topic has three habits. It names the definition or the law before using it, so the examiner can see which result is being applied. It writes each algebraic stage on its own line, so a single slip does not destroy the whole solution and so method marks can be awarded. And it closes with a sentence of interpretation, which is the only place where the difference between describing and inferring becomes visible. A candidate who does those three things consistently will find that the topic yields marks reliably, because the mathematics is short and the vocabulary, once learned, does not change. Every later topic in the subject, from sampling designs through estimation to hypothesis testing, extends exactly one of these steps, so the investment made here is repaid in every subsequent paper.
